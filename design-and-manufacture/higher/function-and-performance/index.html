Function & performance

7 cards · 5 quiz · 2 exam

Design & Manufacture · Higher · mrkellydt.co.uk

Function

Function is what a product has to do. Features such as controls, handles and grips are included to improve it, and function influences the choice of materials, manufacture and assembly.

  • Primary function: the main job (a kettle boils water).
  • Secondary function: extra jobs that add value (water level window, cord storage).

Form follows function: the shape of a product should come from what it has to do (e.g. a funnel's cone, a handle shaped to the grip). Efficient products do their job with the least effort, energy and material.

Performance

How well the product performs its function. Designers consider ease of use, maintenance, life expectancy, fitness for purpose and safety.

  • Fitness for purpose: does it do what it should, for its user, where it will be used, for as long as expected?
  • Maintenance: who will maintain it, what tasks (charge, fill, empty, adjust, replace parts) and how easy access is. Easy-to-maintain products last longer, but can reduce repeat sales.
  • Life expectancy: a disposable razor lasts days; one with a replaceable head lasts years. Longer life usually means better materials and assembly, higher cost and less waste.

Planned obsolescence HIGHER

Designing a product to have a limited life, or to go out of fashion, so it is replaced.

  • Manufacturer: predictable repeat sales, but needs ongoing R&D and risks reputation.
  • Consumer: steady supply of new, affordable products, but constant pressure to upgrade (e.g. phones with batteries that are hard to replace).
  • Environment: more waste and pollution, contributes to a throwaway society.

Style obsolescence: the product still works but looks dated.

Value for money HIGHER

Consumers judge value against similarly priced competitors. Designers research what functions the market expects at a price point, which performance criteria matter most, and how long the product is expected to last.

Key terms

Primary functionThe main job a product is designed to do.
Secondary functionAdditional jobs that add value to the product.
Fitness for purposeHow well a product meets its intended use, user, environment and life expectancy.
Maintenance considerationWho maintains it, what tasks are needed, and how easily parts can be accessed or replaced.
Planned obsolescenceDesigning products with a limited life so they will be replaced.
Style obsolescenceProduct still works but becomes unfashionable.
Value for moneyPerceived quality, function and life compared with similarly priced products.

Quick check

1. A torch that also works as a bottle opener. The bottle opener is…

  1. Secondary function
  2. Primary function
  3. Performance
  4. Aesthetics
Show answer
A: Secondary function. It is an extra job, not the main one.

2. Making a phone battery hard to replace mainly affects which factor?

  1. Aesthetics
  2. Maintenance
  3. Target market
  4. Anthropometrics
Show answer
B: Maintenance. It makes maintenance difficult, pushing replacement instead.

3. Which is a drawback of planned obsolescence for the environment?

  1. Fewer products made
  2. Longer-lasting products
  3. More waste and pollution
  4. Lower energy use
Show answer
C: More waste and pollution. Products are discarded sooner.

4. Which is a benefit of planned obsolescence for a manufacturer?

  1. Fewer sales
  2. Better reputation guaranteed
  3. No need for R&D
  4. Predictable repeat sales
Show answer
D: Predictable repeat sales. Customers need to buy replacements.

5. A razor with replaceable heads compared with a disposable razor has…

  1. Longer life expectancy
  2. Shorter life expectancy
  3. No maintenance
  4. No secondary function
Show answer
A: Longer life expectancy. Replacing only the worn part extends the product's life.

Exam-style questions

1. Describe how the life expectancy of a product can influence its design, manufacture and impact on the environment. (3 marks)

Show marking guidance
  • Long-life products use more durable materials/finishes (1)
  • Assembled so parts can be accessed/replaced, e.g. screws not adhesive (1)
  • Throwaway products use cheaper materials/processes, creating more waste (1)

2. Explain the influence of planned obsolescence on the manufacturer, the consumer and the environment. (3 marks)

Show marking guidance
  • Manufacturer: repeat, predictable sales; but must keep investing in R&D, may lose reputation (1)
  • Consumer: affordable, constant supply of new products; pressure to keep upgrading (1)
  • Environment: increased waste and pollution; throwaway society (1)

Transistors, relays & MOSFETs

9 cards · 8 quiz · 0 exam

Engineering Science · Higher · mrkellydt.co.uk

Transistor as a switch

An NPN transistor has a base, collector and emitter. When the voltage at the base is about 0.7 V or more, the transistor switches on and a larger current flows from collector to emitter, operating the output (a relay, LED, buzzer or motor). A small input current controls a larger output current.

  • A base resistor limits the base current to protect the transistor.
  • A relay lets a low-current control circuit switch a separate, higher-current or higher-voltage output circuit.
  • A protection diode across the relay coil protects the transistor from the high reverse voltage produced when the coil switches off.

BJT current gain HIGHER

hFE = IC ÷ IB. Example: an op-amp output of 4.25 V drives a 1200 Ω base resistor. IB = (4.25 − 0.7) ÷ 1200 = 2.96 mA; with hFE = 320, IC = 0.95 A. Remember the 0.7 V dropped across the base-emitter junction, and that current flowing through a sensor may split between the base and another resistor.

BJT switching calculations step by step HIGHER

Worked example: Vin = Vcc = 9 V, Rb = 150 kΩ, RL = 470 Ω, hFE = 200.

  1. Vbe ≈ 0.7 V, so the voltage across Rb = 9 − 0.7 = 8.3 V.
  2. Ib = 8.3 ÷ 150 000 = 0.0553 mA.
  3. Ic = hFE × Ib = 200 × 0.0553 = 11.1 mA.
  4. VL = Ic × RL = 0.0111 × 470 = 5.2 V.
  5. Vce = Vcc − VL = 9 − 5.2 = 3.8 V.

Designing a base resistor: a 6 V, 60 mA bulb with hFE = 30 needs Ib = 60 ÷ 30 = 2 mA. On a 6 V supply Rb = (6 − 0.7) ÷ 0.002 = 2650 Ω. Choose the next value down (e.g. 2.2 kΩ) so the transistor is driven fully on.

Below about 0.6 V the transistor is off; at 0.7 V it is fully on. In between it is partly on, so an output can flicker. The base–emitter voltage stays at about 0.7 V even if the input rises, because the extra voltage is dropped across Rb.

MOSFETs HIGHER

  • An n-channel enhancement-mode MOSFET is a voltage-operated switch: when the gate-source voltage VGS exceeds the threshold voltage, current flows from drain to source.
  • Almost no current flows into the gate, so it doesn't load the sensing circuit; it has a very low on-resistance, so it wastes little energy as heat and can switch large currents.
  • BJT vs MOSFET: a BJT is current-operated and cheap, good for small loads; a MOSFET is voltage-operated, draws negligible input current and suits high-current loads such as motors and solenoids.
  • Drain-source current: IDS = voltage across the load ÷ load resistance, e.g. (6 − 5.2) ÷ 2.9.

MOSFET calculations and handling HIGHER

  • The drain–source channel is in series with the load, so Vs = VDS + Vload (Kirchhoff's second law).
  • The MOSFET is saturated (fully on) when VDS = VGS − VT. Its on-resistance is then very low, often under 1 Ω.
  • Example: a 470 Ω load on 12 V needs 20 mA, and VT = 2.5 V. Vload = 0.020 × 470 = 9.4 V, so VDS = 12 − 9.4 = 2.6 V and the gate needs VGS = 2.6 + 2.5 = 5.1 V.
  • A variable resistor in a divider can set the gate voltage at the threshold, so a sensor switches the MOSFET on.
  • MOSFETs draw almost no gate current, so they are easily damaged by static electricity: handle them by the case. A MOSFET can replace a relay for high-current loads; unlike a relay it has no moving contacts, so it can switch quickly enough for PWM.

Quick check

1. Vin = 9 V, Rb = 150 kΩ, hFE = 200. Ic is about…

  1. 11 mA
  2. 0.06 mA
  3. 60 mA
  4. 1.2 mA
Show answer
A: 11 mA. Ib = 8.3 ÷ 150 000; Ic = 200 × Ib.

2. A MOSFET (VT = 2.5 V) has VDS = 2.6 V at saturation. VGS is…

  1. 0.1 V
  2. 2.5 V
  3. 5.1 V
  4. 2.6 V
Show answer
C: 5.1 V. VGS = VDS + VT.

3. Why handle MOSFETs by the case?

  1. They get hot
  2. Static can damage the gate
  3. They are fragile glass
  4. To keep them clean
Show answer
B: Static can damage the gate. Negligible gate current: static-sensitive.

  1. 0.07 V

  4. 70 V

B: To protect the transistor from reverse voltage. Back-EMF protection.

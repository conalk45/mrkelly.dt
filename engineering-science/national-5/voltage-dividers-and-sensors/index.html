Voltage dividers & sensors

5 cards · 6 quiz · 0 exam

Engineering Science · National 5 · mrkellydt.co.uk

How a voltage divider works

Two resistances in series across a supply share the voltage in proportion to their resistance: the bigger resistance gets the bigger share.

  • V1 / V2 = R1 / R2
  • V2 = Vs × R2 ÷ (R1 + R2)
  • V1 + V2 = Vs.

Replace one resistor with an LDR or thermistor and the output voltage changes with light or temperature: this is the input signal for a control circuit. Put the sensor at the bottom to make Vout rise when its resistance rises (e.g. LDR at the bottom: Vout rises as it gets darker). Swap them to reverse the action.

Designing a divider

Example: a thermistor of 4.8 kΩ is at the bottom and the transistor needs 1.4 V across the top resistor on a 5 V supply. Vtherm = 5 − 1.4 = 3.6 V; R1/4.8 k = 1.4/3.6, so R1 = 1.9 kΩ. Read sensor resistances from the characteristic graph (resistance against light or temperature) carefully, including the kΩ scale.

Describing a sensing circuit

A full description follows the chain: sensor resistance changes → Vin changes → transistor switches on/off → relay/output switches. E.g. "As the water heats up the thermistor's resistance falls, so Vin rises; the transistor switches on, the relay activates, the blue LED turns off and the red LED turns on." The 2024 report: some didn't refer to the thermistor resistance or Vout.

Adjusting the sensitivity

Replace the fixed resistor with a variable resistor and the circuit can be set to switch at a different light level or temperature: for example an outside light that comes on at dusk in summer and in winter. Changing the variable resistor changes how much of the supply the sensor gets, so it changes the level at which Vout reaches the transistor's switch-on voltage.

Example: an LDR at the bottom of a divider on 9 V, and the transistor must switch on (0.7 V) when the LDR is 2 kΩ. V1 = 9 − 0.7 = 8.3 V, so R1 = 2000 × 8.3 ÷ 0.7 = 23.7 kΩ: set the variable resistor to about 24 kΩ.

To make the circuit work the opposite way (on when light, or on when hot), swap the sensor and the variable resistor.

What catches people out

  • Described a thermistor circuit without mentioning resistance or Vout. "As temperature falls, the thermistor's resistance rises, so Vout rises." N5 2024

Quick check

1. Why replace the fixed resistor in a sensing divider with a variable resistor?

  1. To increase the supply voltage
  2. To adjust the level at which the circuit switches
  3. To protect the LDR
  4. To reverse the current
Show answer
B: To adjust the level at which the circuit switches. It adjusts the sensitivity.

2. An LDR (2 kΩ) is at the bottom of a 9 V divider; the transistor must switch at 0.7 V. The top resistor is about…

  1. 2 kΩ
  2. 24 kΩ
  3. 12 kΩ
  4. 0.16 kΩ
Show answer
B: 24 kΩ. 2000 × 8.3 ÷ 0.7 = 23.7 kΩ.

3. In a divider, the larger resistor has…

  1. The smaller share of voltage
  2. The larger share of voltage
  3. No voltage
  4. The same voltage
Show answer
B: The larger share of voltage. Voltage shares in proportion to resistance.

4. Vs = 9 V, R1 = 1 kΩ, R2 = 2 kΩ. V2 is…

  1. 3 V
  2. 4.5 V
  3. 6 V
  4. 9 V
Show answer
C: 6 V. 9 × 2/3.

5. A thermistor is at the bottom of a divider. As temperature rises, Vout…

  1. Rises
  2. Falls
  3. Stays the same
  4. Reverses
Show answer
B: Falls. Its resistance falls, so its share falls.

6. A good description of a sensing circuit starts with…

  1. The relay
  2. The sensor's resistance changing
  3. The LED
  4. The battery
Show answer
B: The sensor's resistance changing. Follow the chain from the sensor.

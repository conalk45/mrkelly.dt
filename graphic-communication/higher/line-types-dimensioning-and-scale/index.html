Dimensioning rules

  • All sizes in mm; don't write "mm" after each one.
  • Extension lines start with a small gap from the object and run just past the dimension line.
  • Dimension lines are thin, with filled arrowheads touching the extension lines.
  • Numbers sit above the dimension line, readable from the bottom or the right of the sheet.
  • Don't let dimension lines cross; place smaller dimensions nearer the object.
  • Show each size once, in the view that shows that feature most clearly.
  • Don't use outlines or centre lines as dimension lines.

Types of dimension

  • Chain: dimensions follow on from each other, end to end. Shows the size from one feature to the next, but errors can add up.
  • Parallel (datum): all sizes measured from one datum edge, stacked in parallel. Avoids accumulated error.
  • Radius: R10. The leader points from the arc towards its centre.
  • Diameter: Ø20, for circles.
  • Angular: in degrees, with an arc-shaped dimension line.
  • Square: □20 means the feature is square in section.
  • Across flats (A/F) and across corners (A/C): for hexagons.
  • Chamfer: e.g. 4 × 45°.

Tolerances

No part can be made to an exact size, so drawings state the acceptable range.

  • Symmetrical tolerance: 80 ± 0.25.
  • Limits: the maximum and minimum written out, e.g. 82.5 / 80.5. The purpose is that a part within these limits will still fit and work.
  • Common tolerance: a note in the title block such as "all tolerances ± 0.5 unless stated".

Questions may ask for the maximum and minimum limits: add and subtract the tolerance.

Scale

Scale compares the drawing size with the real size.

  • 1:1 full size. 1:2, 1:5, 1:10 reductions (the drawing is smaller). 2:1, 5:1 enlargements, for small parts or detail views.
  • To find a drawn size: real size ÷ scale (1:5 → divide by 5). To find a real size from an enlargement of 5:1, divide the drawn size by 5.
  • Choose a scale big enough to show detail and allow clear dimensions; examiners criticised assignment drawings that were too small to read.

Key terms

Centre line
Thin chain line: long dash, short dash.
Cutting plane line
Thin chain, thick at the ends, with arrows and letters.
Fold line
Thin chain line with two short dashes, used on surface developments.
Chain dimensioning
Dimensions end to end; shows feature-to-feature sizes but errors accumulate.
Parallel (datum) dimensioning
All sizes from one datum; avoids accumulated error.
□20 on a dimension
The feature is square in section.
A/F
Across flats: the size between opposite flat sides of a hexagon.
Scale 5:1
The drawing is five times bigger than the real object.
Limits
The maximum and minimum allowable sizes so the part still fits and works.
Symmetrical tolerance
e.g. 16 ± 0.25: the size may vary by 0.25 either way.

Quick check

1. Which line type shows the centre of a circle?

  1. Thin dashed
  2. Thin chain
  3. Continuous thick
  4. Continuous thin
Show answer
B: Thin chain. Centre lines are thin chain.

2. Dimensions placed end to end are called…

  1. Parallel
  2. Chain
  3. Datum
  4. Angular
Show answer
B: Chain. Chain dimensioning.

3. Which avoids the build-up of errors?

  1. Chain dimensioning
  2. Parallel (datum) dimensioning
  3. Leader lines
  4. Hidden detail
Show answer
B: Parallel (datum) dimensioning. All sizes from one datum.

4. On a drawing at 1:5, a 40 mm line represents…

  1. 8 mm
  2. 40 mm
  3. 200 mm
  4. 45 mm
Show answer
C: 200 mm. Real size = 40 × 5.

5. The symbol □ before a dimension means…

  1. Cubed
  2. Square section
  3. Area
  4. Approximate
Show answer
B: Square section. Do not write "cubed".

6. A dimension is 81.5 ± 1. The maximum limit is…

  1. 80.5
  2. 81.5
  3. 82.5
  4. 83
Show answer
C: 82.5. 81.5 + 1.

7. Dimension figures should be readable from…

  1. Any direction
  2. The bottom or right of the sheet
  3. The top
  4. The left only
Show answer
B: The bottom or right of the sheet. BS convention.

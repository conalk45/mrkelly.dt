Notes

Why research first?

Good ideas come from understanding the problem. Research stops you designing for a problem that doesn't exist, and gives you facts, people and inspiration to build on.

Start by turning the brief into questions: who is it for? What do they struggle with now? What already exists? What are the limits (budget, size, time, materials)?

Secondary research

Secondary research uses information other people have already gathered:

  • websites, articles, reports and statistics
  • books, documentaries and podcasts
  • existing products, campaigns and buildings (case studies)
  • image research and mood boards for visual inspiration

Always note where it came from and ask whether the source is reliable, current and unbiased.

Primary research

Primary research is new information you collect yourself:

  • Interviews: in-depth answers from users or experts; ask open questions ("Tell me about…").
  • Surveys/questionnaires: views from lots of people quickly; mix closed and open questions.
  • Observation: watch how people actually behave, which is often different from what they say.
  • Photographs and site visits: record the real place or situation.
  • Trying it yourself: use the existing product or service and note what's frustrating.

Making research count

  • Analyse, don't just collect. After each piece, write what it tells you and what it means for your ideas ("This shows… so I need to…").
  • Summarise key findings: a list of insights or a "problem statement".
  • Use it to write a "How might we…?" question that guides your ideas.
  • Show research visually: annotated images, charts from your survey, quotes from interviews.

Copied pages with no comment show very little thinking.

Key terms

Secondary research
Information others have already gathered: websites, books, case studies
Primary research
New information you collect: interviews, surveys, observation
Mood board
A collage of images, colours and textures for visual inspiration
Case study (in research)
Looking closely at an existing product, campaign or place
Open question
Can't be answered yes or no: "Tell me about…"
Analysing research
Writing what it tells you and what it means for your ideas
Reliable source
Trustworthy, up to date and not biased
Problem statement
A short summary of the real problem your research has found

Quick check

1. Which is secondary research?

  1. Interviewing a user
  2. Reading a report online
  3. Observing people at a bus stop
  4. Running a survey
Show answer
B: Reading a report online. It uses information someone else gathered.

2. Which is primary research?

  1. Watching a documentary
  2. Reading a magazine
  3. Interviewing a nurse about hospital waiting rooms
  4. Looking at a website
Show answer
C: Interviewing a nurse about hospital waiting rooms. You collected it yourself.

3. What turns collected research into useful research?

  1. Printing more pages
  2. Analysing what it means for your ideas
  3. Using larger images
  4. Adding colour
Show answer
B: Analysing what it means for your ideas. Comment on what it shows and how it affects your ideas.

4. Which question is open?

  1. Do you like the park?
  2. How often do you visit?
  3. Tell me about your last visit to the park
  4. Is the park clean?
Show answer
C: Tell me about your last visit to the park. It invites a full answer.

5. Why use observation as well as surveys?

  1. It's quicker
  2. People often do something different from what they say
  3. It needs no planning
  4. It gives statistics
Show answer
B: People often do something different from what they say. Observation shows real behaviour.
